The gap between imagining something and having it built is collapsing. You think about it, you describe it, and it exists. An app, a workflow, a storefront  -  live in minutes, not months. The speed of building software is approaching the speed of describing what you want.

A grandmother in Thanjavur who wants an app to do bhajans and book temple offerings. A farmer in Vidarbha whose entire interface is a voice button  -  he speaks in Marathi, asks for cotton prices across three mandis, and gets an answer. A coaching class teacher in Patna who wants parents to book slots, pay fees, and get progress reports  -  all in Hindi.

I don't know what 1.4 billion Indians want. All I do know is it will be possible for every Indian to have an app of their choice, their UI, their preferred functionality. The building blocks for a billion hyperlocal, voice-first, vernacular apps exist today. Hyperpersonalisation is now possible for apps because the costs are that low and will keep dropping.

Think about what this actually means. Software was once the moat. You needed engineering teams, capital, months of development. That moat is evaporating. A street food vendor in Madurai can have an app as polished as a Series B startup. A self-help group in rural Bihar can have their own lending interface. Not someone else's app that they squeeze into  -  their own. Built around how they think, how they work, how their customers find them. Every individual, every small business, becomes their own platform.

Now, a world of a billion apps sounds beautiful in theory. But apps alone are just interfaces  -  pretty boxes with nothing inside. What makes an app useful is what it connects you to. The services, the transactions, the commerce flowing through it.

When apps are abundant, what's the differentiator? The services you can access through each. Today, maybe the best sellers are on Amazon and hence most buyers buy on Amazon, which in turn brings more sellers on Amazon. Creates this virtuous cycle, often vicious cycle, where both sides are plagued by predatory policies. But if I can access my customers through my app, built for me  -  why would I even be on Amazon? I set the terms. I am in control of my earnings and my destiny. Similarly for a buyer. **This is the vision Beckn powers.**

Beckn started with a deceptively simple question  -  why is it so hard for a consumer and a provider of any value to discover each other, trust each other, and exchange value directly? Why do we always need somebody in the centre to facilitate this? We have language for communication  -  peer-to-peer, no intermediary. We have protocols for the internet  -  any browser, any device, any website. We have UPI for payments  -  any app, any bank, one network. But for commerce, for services, for mobility  -  we're still resigned to the idea that you need one big central platform to bring both sides together. Beckn flips this model. It's a protocol and an infrastructure  -  that lets a consumer on any app and a provider on any app discover each other, contract, transact, and fulfil, with nobody in the centre.

A protocol and infrastructure like Beckn is even more powerful in the AI era where everyone can create apps. Their own apps. How they want. It doesn't matter if there are a thousand apps or a billion  -  every one of them can tap into the same open network. The app becomes the interface. Beckn becomes the economy underneath.

Think of our earlier examples - Each of these apps is only valuable if it can *reach the rest of the world* - temples, mandis, payment providers, logistics, service providers. Without that, they're just isolated pieces of software.

Beckn ensures that these hyper-personal, locally built apps don't live in isolation. A grandmother's puja app can discover temples offering rituals. A farmer's voice app can query mandis already on the network. A teacher's app can collect fees and coordinate services without being locked into a single platform. Beckn turns a billion personalised apps into a single, interoperable economy.

Below are some familiar pictures from India. There's a pattern here that I think is deeply Indian and deeply underappreciated. Every time a new technology arrives that solves a deep need, a local entrepreneur figures out how to make it accessible to the neighbourhood. They don't wait for permission. They don't wait for subsidies. They just set up shop.

![STD booths and cyber cafes](posts/speed-of-thought-1.png) ![AI cyber cafes](posts/speed-of-thought-2.png)

**First era** - STD/PCO booths. Someone on your street corner put up a yellow sign and charged you per minute to make a phone call. Close to a million booths at their peak, employing over a million people. An entire industry that sprang from nothing  -  not because of some government scheme, but because one person saw that their neighbours needed to make phone calls and set up shop.

**Second era** - Cyber cafes. Someone in your locality crammed five monitors into a small room and charged you by the hour. You checked your email, filled your exam form, and printed your bus ticket. Still around, still serving people. Same pattern  -  a local entrepreneur turning new technology into neighbourhood access.

**Third era** - AI cyber cafes. Similar one-man shops where you walk in, describe the app you want, and walk out with it built for ₹150. This is not that far away. The local entrepreneur doesn't need to know how to code. They just need to know their customers and a prompt.

Technology changes. The entrepreneur stays. Every era, they figure it out.

Imagine, millions of local builders creating apps for 1.4 billion people  -  and because those apps are Beckn-enabled, 1.4 billion people can discover, transact, and exchange value directly with each other.
